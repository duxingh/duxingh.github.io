*The Problems of Philosophy* is a 1912 book by philosopher Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) in which he attempts to provide a brief, accessible guide to philosophical problems.

## Chapter I. Appearance and Reality

The author tells us that the most important goal in philosophy is to determine which knowledge is certain.

Then Russell shows that even the existence of an ordinary thing—such as a table—is not as certain as it appears. We know a table by its **appearance**: color, hardness, sound, and shape, but these "sense-data" may change if the environment or the point of view changes. So what is the **reality** of a table?

Bishop Berkeley (1685–1753) says that the reality of a table is the mind of God; Leibniz (1646–1716) says it is a collection of rudimentary minds. Both admit the existence of the table but deny the existence of matter. A physicist would say that the reality of a table is a cloud of electric charges. Comparing their answers, none makes the reality clear to us.
