*The Problems of Philosophy* is a 1912 book by philosopher Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) in which he attempts to provide a brief, accessible guide to philosophical problems.

## Chapter I. Appearance and Reality

The author tells us that the most important goal in philosophy is to determine which knowledge is certain.

Then Russell shows that even the existence of an ordinary thing—such as a table—is not as certain as it appears. We know a table by its **appearance**: color, hardness, sound, and shape, but these "sense-data" may change if the environment or the point of view changes. So what is the **reality** of a table?

Bishop Berkeley (1685–1753) says that the reality of a table is the mind of God; Leibniz (1646–1716) says it is a collection of rudimentary minds. Both admit the existence of the table but deny the existence of matter. A physicist would say that the reality of a table is a cloud of electric charges. Comparing their answers, none makes the reality clear to us.

## Chapter II. The Existence of Matter

To answer the question of whether matter exists, the author begins with Descartes' (1596–1650) basic principle. Descartes thinks that everything is doubtful until we carefully prove that it is certain. From all doubtful knowledge he finds out one thing he cannot deny: *he is thinking.* This is the foundation of Descartes' philosophy.

Let us adopt Descartes' idea. We can't be sure if a table exists, but we can be sure the sense-data of it exist since we're thinking through these sense-data. Now we're facing two choices: supposing these sense-data correspond to a reality or supposing that there is no matter. Both of them are logically possible.

From a purely practical standpoint, accepting the existence of matter is beneficial; the achievements of natural science prove this. However, this is still insufficient to convince those who refuse to accept the existence of matter, because they would argue that natural science is nothing more than sense-data transmitted to you by God.
